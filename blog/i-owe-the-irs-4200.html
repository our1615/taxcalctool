I Owe the IRS $4,200 Because I Trusted TurboTax's 'Maximum Refund' Promise

I made a mistake. I need to say that first, because what follows involves a CPA, a tax software commercial, and a level of embarrassment that can only be achieved by someone who should know better.

I filed my taxes in April using TurboTax. The software said I was getting a $2,800 refund. I was thrilled. I planned the refund. I allocated it. $1,000 to credit card debt. $800 to my emergency fund. $1,000 to a weekend trip to Big Bend. I hit "file" and waited.

The letter arrived in June. Not a refund check. A letter. From the IRS. "We have adjusted your return. You owe $4,200." I read it three times. I thought it was a scam. I called the IRS. I was on hold for 2 hours and 17 minutes. When I finally got through, the representative confirmed it. The letter was real. I owed $4,200.

Here's what happened. TurboTax had asked me a question: "Did you receive any unemployment compensation in 2025?" I had. For 8 weeks. $8,400 total. I entered the amount. TurboTax calculated the tax. But it didn't calculate the penalty for underpayment. Because I hadn't made quarterly estimated payments on the unemployment income. And it didn't flag that my withholding from my regular job was insufficient to cover the additional income.

I called my friend who is a CPA in Austin. I told her the numbers. She was not surprised. "Michael, tax software is a calculator. It's not a strategist. It calculates what you tell it. It doesn't tell you what you should have done differently." She explained that unemployment income is taxable at the federal level. In Texas, there's no state income tax, so that part was fine. But the federal tax on $8,400 at my marginal rate was $1,848. Plus the underpayment penalty of $185. Plus interest of $67. Total: $2,100. But the IRS also disallowed my home office deduction because I hadn't kept proper records. That added another $2,100. Total owed: $4,200.

I felt like an idiot. I'm not a tax expert, but I'm not financially illiterate either. I have a bachelor's degree. I run a website about tax calculators. And I had just been blindsided by a tax bill because I trusted software to think for me.

Here's what I learned. Tax software is a tool. It's not a replacement for understanding. The "Maximum Refund" promise is marketing. It's not a guarantee. It's an algorithm that optimizes within the inputs you provide. If you don't know to input something — like quarterly estimated payments, or proper home office documentation — the software can't help you.

My CPA friend helped me set up a plan. I filed an amended return. I paid the $4,200. I set up quarterly estimated payments for 2026. I started tracking my home office expenses in a dedicated spreadsheet — square footage, utilities, internet, repairs. I bought a separate filing system for tax documents. And I scheduled a mid-year tax review for July.

The mid-year review was eye-opening. By June, I had already earned 60% of my annual income. My withholding was based on my salary, not my side income. The tax software wouldn't catch this until April of next year. But my CPA caught it in July. She calculated that I needed to increase my quarterly payments by $400. I did it. I adjusted my budget. And when I file in 2027, I won't owe. I might even get a small refund.

If you use tax software — and most of us do — please do this. Run a mid-year estimate. Not in April. In July. Check your withholding. Check your estimated payments. Check your deductions. If you have multiple income sources, if you're self-employed, if you have investment income, you need to check more than once a year. The software won't remind you. The IRS will. But their reminder comes with a bill.

— Michael, from a desk in Austin where the quarterly payments are now automated and the home office is properly documented
